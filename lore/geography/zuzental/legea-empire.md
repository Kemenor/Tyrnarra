# Legea Empire (Zuzental)

*Written under the [`region-prose.md`](../../../docs/region-prose.md) contract (2026-09-29). Parent domain: [`../zuzental.md`](../zuzental.md). The Faith's god and his secret: this file, *The Faith*; the keeper of his debt: [`../bolverk.md`](../bolverk.md), *Jafnar*.*

**Etymology:** *Legea* is the ground's name, older than the Empire; the Knight's camp took it, and the Faith's holy words share its root. The capital is **Debreqal**; the needle peaks above it, **Orratzak**. Demonym: **Legean**. (Full derivations in the glossary.)

**Position:** north-western Zuzental, on the Midarra coast. The Hegandi escarpment walls the north, with the Vernua Dominion above it; the Thousand Kingdom lies east, across the line where House Vester holds Hagwold; the Namur Republic lies south-west and the Order of Law south. The eastern half of **Hringseyja**, across the Quietline, is Legean.

**Terrain:** lowland farm country across most of its breadth: river valleys and long rolling ground, woods along the rivers, heath and pasture on the higher ground. Rivers come down off the escarpment to a lake in the middle of the country, with low hills east of it; a river runs the southern border to a second lake at the Thousand Kingdom corner. In the south-west three sheer needles of grey rock stand straight out of the lowland: the **Orratzak**, with Debreqal at their eastern foot and the high reading-house on the tallest.

**Character:** the answer is already written.

**Peoples:** **Humans**, at their fullest expression on Talan, some three and a half million in about six thousand parishes; Elves along the Thousand Kingdom line and Hobgoblins along the Namur border, in smaller numbers; converts from every domain, received under new names.

**Tongue:** Talanese; the Faith's holy words are older than the Empire. *(The local tongue is pending the regional-tongue pass.)*

**Faith:** the Divine Faith of **Legaun**, a god of the Postlife layer.

**Rule:** a hereditary demigod theocracy: the demigod of the Knight's line over the **Irakul**, the readers of the **Legedi**, with a reading-house in every parish.

**Founded:** the first third of the Dark Era, as the Knight's camp under the Orratzak (c. 1370 MR).

## The book at the door

> "I asked the reader at the river-house whether the cask was mine or the innkeeper's, since he'd drunk half of it and paid for none. She asked me two things and him three, she had the book open before he'd finished lying, and she read out the line. He paid me on the step, and I was back on the road by noon. In Lograth I'd still be looking for a bench."
> Eoan Darragh Ard Liss, a cask-carter of the Vernua Dominion, on the road down the Hegandi

Every village in Legea has a reading-house, and every city quarter has several: a stone hall with one door, a lectern facing it, and on the shelves behind the lectern a full copy of the **Legedi**, the god's book, in sixty-two volumes. A reader of the **Irakul** sits at the door from first light to dusk, and anyone may bring a question, Legean or foreign.

The questions are ordinary life. Whose is the ox that walked into the barley. Whether a widow may marry her husband's brother, and in which season. What a thief owes, and in what coin. Which field lies fallow this year. The book holds the rule and the reader establishes the facts: she asks the parties and the witnesses what happened, then finds the page that says what follows from it, and reads the line aloud. The answer is law from that moment. The house carries it out, a debt paid before the asker leaves the step, a thief's penalty done by sundown. A lie told at the door stands on the heaviest page in the harvest volume, and Legeans tell the reader the truth more readily than they tell their mothers. There are no advocates at a reading-house and no second hearing.

> "The foreigners think I decide. I have sixty-two volumes and a line at the door from dawn, and I have not had an opinion at this lectern in twenty years. I find."
> Kokeb Irakul ye-Meda, reader of a village house on the central lake

A reader receives her **Deia**, the life the book gives every Legean at fifteen, like anyone else, and spends twenty years learning where the answers lie before she sits a door alone. The good ones have the page open before the question is finished; the best know which questions are really two questions, and read both. A Legean quotes the page the way a Lograth advocate quotes precedent (*forty-one of the harvest volume, the third line*), and the reader-schools of Debreqal argue in private over which page a hard case belongs to. Outsiders who have watched two readers find two different pages for the same question have written it down.

**The day's page.** At first light every reading-house in the Empire opens the same page and reads it aloud at the door. The day's page holds the day's ordinance, what the day is for and what is eaten on it, which roads carry carts and which fields are entered, and its **litany**, the names that belong to that day. After it the reader calls the names that have come free in the house since yesterday. From the Orratzak to the Quietline the whole Empire hears the same words within the same hour, and a Legean caravan on a foreign road halts at dawn for the dawn prayer.

**The Unwritten.** A question the village reader cannot place goes up by page-rider to Debreqal, to the senior readers of the high reading-house, who search the whole book for it; most come back down within the month with a page and a line. What the high house cannot find in any volume is a new *kind* of question: a new kind of plough, a sickness no one has named, the first Magitrain surveyors asking to cross the plain. Those alone go to the demigod, a few dozen in a year, and each answer is written into the master Legedi as a new line under the page it belongs to. Each spring the high house gathers the year's lines onto new leaves and copies them once for each of the Empire's six thousand houses; the page-riders carry the leaves out, and each reader binds them into her Legedi where the high house directs. The book has taken some thirty thousand new lines since the Knight's day and grown from forty volumes to sixty-two, and a reader can date a line by the hand it was copied in.

**What the book leaves unasked.** The Legedi holds a page for every question between mortals. It holds no page for a question against the throne: what the demigod owes, what the high house has taken, what a governor did on his own account. A Legean who brings one to the door is told gently that it is not a question, and the reader writes nothing down.

**◈ Popular Belief: the sixty-third volume.** Every Legean knows the Legedi runs to sixty-two volumes, and every Legean has heard of the sixty-third. It holds the answers to the questions against the throne, the story goes, and it is too dangerous to copy: one book, kept sealed at the high house, read only by the demigod and the First Reader's innermost circle. Readers deny it with a smile. Children dare each other to ask for it at the door.

**⚿ GM Secret: the sixty-third volume is blank.** The volume exists. It stands on its own shelf in the high reading-house, bound like the others, sealed, and guarded by the First Reader's circle, who have kept its rumour alive for eight centuries because a book that might hold the answers keeps a people from looking for them anywhere else. Each demigod opens it once, alone, on the night of the raising, and finds its pages empty. None of the five has written in it. The only book in creation that holds those answers is the one Jafnar keeps in the Counting House: the First Account, the people's ledger against Legaun.

**The latch-night.** The book gives each Legean a Deia at fifteen, and the page is on the shelf the night before. The young go to the reading-house in the dark to read their own page ahead of the morning; readers leave the latch undone that night, and most of them went themselves at fifteen. Some of the young come out and walk straight home to tell their mothers. Some sit with their page until morning.

## What a Legean is called

A Legean carries three names: **day-name, Deia, and reading-house**. A child under fifteen carries two.

The **day-name** is written for you. Each day's page holds a litany of ten names for daughters and ten for sons, drawn from a common stock of some twelve hundred names; each name stands on the litanies of several days across the year. A newborn is entered at the reading-house and receives **the first name on its birth-day's litany that no living member of that house carries**. The reader reads the name out at the door, and then whose it was: *you carry the name of Kokeb the herder, who drowned in the spring flood*. No Legean chooses a day-name, and it does not change; a day-name and its house are unique among the living faithful.

**How a name comes free.** Every house keeps its own copy of the litany pages, consecrated at the house's founding, and on them each name carried by a living member of the house stands dark. A name comes free in one of two ways: its bearer dies, or its bearer leaves the Faith. Either way the ink pales, wherever in the world it happens, and the reader sees it when she opens the pages at dawn. The book draws no line between the two. A Legean who renounces Legaun is dead to the house on the morning the ink pales, and the next child born on the day takes the name while its last bearer is still walking about in the Thousand Kingdom. A Legean who comes back to the Faith is received like any convert, under a new name.

So a Legean house learns of a death before any letter can reach it. A mother whose son went to the Guild watches his name every morning; a reader sometimes has to walk to a farm before the Guild's rider does. And a name that stays dark tells the house its bearer lives, whatever the letter says.

> "We had the letter from the Guild in the spring: fallen at the Stitchery, no body recovered. His mother went to the reading-house every morning after, and every morning his name was dark. She stopped wearing grey in the summer. He came home the next spring with one arm and a Vanara wife."
> Kebede Negade ye-Wenz, of his wife's brother

**⚿ GM Secret: what the ink sees.** Legaun sees his followers and nothing else, and the litany pages show his sight. The ink pales when the believer leaves it: at death, when the soul passes into the Postlife layer, and at loss of faith, which the page takes the day it happens, spoken or not. A Legean who stops believing and tells no one has a pale name on the morning after, and the reader of the house knows it before the Legean's own family. Readers call this a *quiet paling* and keep it to themselves, as far as the high house knows. The undead of the faithful keep their names dark, and so does a soul held somewhere short of the Postlife layer.

The **Deia**, the god's call, comes at fifteen, when the book gives each Legean a life: farmer, weaver, merchant, soldier, scribe, reader. The trade-word becomes the middle name. A Legean who asks the book for a new life takes the new Deia into the name the same day (see *Asking for a new life*); former Deia stay on the page and leave the name. Marriage changes nothing.

The **reading-house** closes the name with *ye-*, "of": the house that keeps your page. A country Legean's house is the village's (*ye-Meda*, of the field; *ye-Wenz*, of the river); a city Legean's is a few streets (*ye-Gedel* in Debreqal, the cliff streets). Houses stay the size of a parish, near six hundred souls. A hamlet under about a hundred and fifty belongs to the next village's house and walks there for every entry; a large village keeps two, the upper street's and the lower's; a city quarter holds several. When a newborn finds all ten names on its day's litany held, the house **splits**: the book is copied, the litany pages consecrated anew, and the new house takes half the streets or half the village. The split comes to a growing house by twelve hundred souls at the latest, and it is the one time a family's last name changes.

A **convert** is received on a day and named for it: the first free name on the litany of the day of reception, so a Legean hears in a convert's day-name the day they came in. The **demigods** set all three names down at their raising and take a **throne-name** of their own choosing, the only chosen names in the Empire. An heir's Deia is *Worash*.

In use a Legean is the day-name at home, day-name and Deia at work, and all three on paper.

**Sample names.** Day-names for daughters: Tsehai, Kokeb, Lemlem, Tsega, Hiwot, Mulu, Selam, Aster, Yeshi, Alem. For sons: Tesfa, Berhan, Abeb, Kebede, Tades, Desta, Tamrat, Negash, Alemu, Fikre. Deia: Gebere (farmer), Shemane (weaver), Negade (merchant), Wetadr (soldier), Tsehafi (scribe), Eregna (herder), Irakul (reader), Worash (heir). Houses: ye-Meda, ye-Wenz, ye-Gedel, ye-Amba, ye-Kolla, ye-Orratzak. Whole: *Tsehai ye-Meda* · *Tsehai Shemane ye-Meda* · *Kebede Negade ye-Wenz* · *Kokeb Irakul ye-Meda* · *Tesfa Worash ye-Orratzak* · *Amdeqal*.

> "I carry old Tsehai the dyer's name. My mother says I sneeze like her."
> Tsehai Shemane ye-Meda, a weaver of the central lake

**◈ Popular Belief: the name remembers.** Folk say a name brings something of its last bearer along: a laugh, a knack with bees, a sneeze. Grandmothers watch the newborns who take the names of the village's great talkers and great cooks, and a family that draws the name of a renouncer keeps a close eye on the child.

## Asking for a new life

> "I was a soldier eleven years. One morning I stood at the door and asked what now, and the reader said merchant, and I went home and sold my boots."
> Kebede Negade ye-Wenz, a grain-merchant of the river-house

Any Legean past fifteen may stand at the door on any morning and ask the great question: *what life now?* The book answers with a Deia, the old one closes that day, and the new one goes into the name. Most Legeans ask twice in a working life. Some ask five times, and the river-houses know a boatwright who has asked nine. No reader has ever asked again.

This is where the Humans of Talan live at full strength: the ones who force the question. A Legean decides in the time a Thousand Kingdom family takes to sit down to decide, because the deciding is already done and waits on a shelf. A farmer who hears of drought upstream is at the door by noon, and by evening half the valley has been to the door after her.

The pull reaches over the borders. People tired of waiting come to be received: Thousand Kingdom families worn out by litigated oaths, Vernuan farmers from over the Hegandi, Fenurran tribesfolk since the missionaries went north.

## Debreqal and the needles

Debreqal stands at the eastern foot of the Orratzak, a city of about seventy-five thousand in some hundred and twenty-five reading-houses, and it began as the Knight's camp in the notch between the needles and the plain, where one wall and the rock could hold against the spawn. The first reading-house still stands in the notch, the smallest in the city.

The **high reading-house** stands on the tallest needle, reached by a stair cut into the rock and a basket-lift for the page-riders' loads. It keeps the **master Legedi**, from which every copy in the Empire descends, and the seat of the demigod beside it; the **First Reader**, chief of the Irakul, reads at its door. Below, the city lives on the book: the **copying-halls** of the lower town, parchment-yards along the river, binders and ink-makers and the stables of the page-riders. Each spring's leaves pass through the halls in a season, six thousand copies in the same hand, and a copyist's mark in the margin tells a reader which hall and which year a leaf came from.

## The line

The founder is **Lezain**, the name the god gave him. The Dark-Era refugee records, kept by those who survived the camp and copied later into the Guild's early archives, call him **Osric Hale**, a man-at-arms of no Imperial house, who held the notch under the needles against the spawn from about 1370 MR. The camp became a town, the town a kingdom, and the Knight rose a demigod within his own lifetime. He died around 1620, and the throne passed within his line.

Five demigods have reigned. Each takes a throne-name at the raising and sets down the names the book gave them. The fifth, **Amdeqal**, was raised in 2290 and has reigned two hundred and forty-two years.

**The heir.** The heir is a mortal of the line, and the book names the heir: the demigod answers the heir-page as an Unwritten question, and the First Reader reads it at the high house. An heir waits a mortal lifetime and often dies waiting. Amdeqal's son was named heir at her raising and died in 2376; her grandson was named in his place and died in 2511; the heir now is her grandson's son, **Tesfa Worash ye-Orratzak**, named at twenty-one and forty-two this year, the third heir of her reign. He is unmarried, and the book has named no match.

**The One Reading.** When a demigod dies, every reading-house in the Empire opens the heir-page at the same dawn and reads it at the door, and the heir rises a demigod within the rite. The Faith teaches that the god confirms the heir at that hour. Scholars outside the Empire note that the heir rises during the reading hour itself, in every account of the five, and that the Knight rose the same way on the belief of his refugees, the ascent the Faith's doctrine says the line never needed. The demigods differ in strength from reign to reign, and the difference follows the size of the Empire more closely than the generation: Amdeqal, raised by the largest Empire yet, is held the strongest since the Knight.

**⚿ GM Secret: the blood does nothing.** The Knight's blood carries nothing divine. A demigod of the line is raised the way the Knight was, by belief (the Mortal → Demi-God rung, `../../cosmology.md`, *The Mortal Ascent Ladder*): at the One Reading six thousand reading-houses name one person to the whole Empire at the same hour, and the Empire's belief lifts whoever the page names. Legaun recognises the raised and works through them in the Compact's permitted forms; he confers nothing and has no need to. Any mortal read at the One Reading would rise.

So the throne sits where the heir-page is read. The demigod writes the answer; the First Reader reads it, alone, at the high house, and every copy of the heir-page is copied from her reading. A false reading at the One Reading would raise whomever it named, and the Empire would believe it had watched the god confirm the line. Amdeqal knows how she rose; she felt it arrive house by house through the reading hour. The present First Reader suspects. The Faith's clergy below the needles believe the doctrine.

## The Faith

The Faith names its god openly: **Legaun**. It publishes nothing of what he was and names no home plane among the three of the Postlife layer (Elysium, Diyu, Abyss); asked, the clergy answer that *the teaching is the same in all three*. Legaun is sovereign in the Postlife layer, and on the Life Layer he acts through the Compact's permitted forms: his clergy, his chosen instrument, the demigod of the line, the consecrated page (`../../cosmology.md`, *Scope*).

What sets the Faith apart on Talan is that its god writes how mortals live: ritual, calendar, marriage, contract, punishment, the day, and the Irakul read it out at the door. In the rest of Zuzental mortals write their own laws, and Forseti's Lawspeakers judge and witness them.

The Faith teaches that the demigod's authority comes from the god directly and runs downward, a relay of divine will with no source in the governed. Its own founding tells the other story: the Knight rose on the belief of the people he protected, and every heir since has risen at a reading the whole Empire hears. The Faith holds that the Knight was always the god's chosen and his divinity a recognition. Scholars outside the Empire prefer the story of the refugees.

**Reach.** The Faith sends readers abroad. It keeps small reading-houses in several Zuzental towns, lawful because private worship breaks no mortal-written statute of Zuzental, and one in Hagwold for the Auben. Its missionaries have gone as far north as Fenurra and converted a portion of the tribes (see [`../ehizahar/fenurra.md`](../ehizahar/fenurra.md), *The schism*).

**⚿ GM Secret: what the Faith does not know about Legaun.** The Faith names its god openly: **Legaun** (Basque *legejaun*, "law-lord"). What it does not know is what he was. Legaun was a god-king of the mid Gods' Era, banished to Layer 3 at the forging of the Gods' Law; the Knight's ground is the old court's ground, and the *lege*-rooted names of this country descend from the law-lord's own vocabulary. The clergy's *the teaching is the same in all three* is the god declining to have an address: somewhere in Layer 3 a creditor keeps the people's unpaid ledger against him (**the First Account**), and its keeper is **Jafnar**, the Justice Virtue Devil of Bolverk, once Legaun's own clerk. Whether the Empire is the god rebuilding an estate or attempting penance is deliberately double-readable; the ledger does not care. The Legedi's missing page against the throne is the old court's rule carried over: in Legaun's court, too, the crown's own takings had no hearing. Full canon: [`../bolverk.md`](../bolverk.md), *Jafnar: the Justice Virtue Devil → ⚿ GM Secret: the First Account*.

## Across the line

> "Half my cousins say the reading closed their oath to the Crown. The Crown says an oath closes when it is released. I say come to dinner, and they come, and we talk about the harvest."
> Joscelin Mathe Vestermond, head of House Vester, at Hagwold

Legea and the rest of Zuzental meet at the Thousand Kingdom line and at every market where a Legean trades, and the two laws meet with them.

**Oaths.** In Zuzental an oath binds because it was sworn, until it is released. In Legea an oath binds as far as a page allows it, and reception closes every oath a convert swore before. A Legean does not swear a Standing Oath at Lograth; the Empire holds such an oath void, and Lograth holds it for life.

**Contracts.** Every Legean sale, lease, and loan is made on the Legedi's own forms, and a clause the forms do not hold is void in a reading-house. Vernuan cask-carters and Kingdom armourers who sell into the Empire sell by the page.

**Crime.** A Legean thief is judged at the door and punished by sundown, with no second hearing. The Thousand Kingdom returns no fugitive to a court without appeal, and the Empire enters no Kingdom verdict against a received convert.

**Marriage and inheritance.** A Legean marriage is made by reading; a Kingdom marriage by oath before witnesses. When an Auben marries a Legean by reading, the Kingdom's heralds see no marriage and the reading-house sees no other, and the Hagwold fields at the border change hands in two registers at once.

**The Quietline.** Itsasalda holds the line across Hringseyja as sworn. The Empire holds it as a page, entered by the governor who stood at the swearing, binding because it is written; a later page could write it otherwise.

**Live tension.** The next Re-Swearing at Lograth. When the Thousand Kingdom's new monarch crosses to the Scales, House Vester re-swears its charter, and the Auben hold that their reception closed their part of that oath; the Crown holds that it stands. Some Auben have gone back on the Faith to keep their place in the house, and in the Hagwold reading-house their names are pale. The Empire watches the Kingdom's succession for a weak reign. To the south-west, the Empire fought the **Namur Republic** from 2398 to 2402; the sworn peace since has opened Portoferma, at the mouth of the Empire's river, to Legean trade, and the Empire has offered its army against the Blight-Seer's gate in Namur's hills (see [`namur-republic.md`](namur-republic.md)).
