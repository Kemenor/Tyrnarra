# Tvisol: the Kingdom of the Two Suns (Earth · Sarrum / Darkness · Araphel)

**Etymology:** Icelandic *tví-* (two, twin) + *sól* (sun) → *Tvísól*, accent-loss drift → **Tvisol**, "the two suns." The deep-old name of the corner coast, reclaimed at the kingdom's refounding in the usual Talanese way (kingdoms inherit names). The continent's maps carry the two domain-facing names for its halves: **Twin Suns** (the Brauogi half, a plain modern translation of Tvisol) and **Bikitsa** (the Myrkono half, "twin-shadow"; see [Myrkono](../myrkono.md)). Where Brauogi names what the two suns bring (light, harvest, gold), Myrkono names what they cast.

**Position:** the corner where three countries meet: Brauogi's gold lowlands running in from the north and east, Myrkono's shadow-hills (Izarelai's pilot-coast) from the west, and Floteyn's drifting Isles of Shuun across the water to the south. The kingdom fronts the **Midarra only**. The domain line between Sarrum's ground and Araphel's runs through the middle of the kingdom to the sea; the **Soul Tree** island stands offshore east, close enough to see from any roof of the sunward coast.

---

On the first morning of Guestide the whole kingdom is on its roofs. Solyra rises where she always rises; then, low over the eastern water where the Soul Tree island rides, a second light stands up out of the sea, dimmer than the sun, warmer than either moon, and stays. For a few weeks each year this corner coast has two lights in its sky, two shadows under every cart and gull, and a second crop ripening in fields that already gave one. The continent's chronicles call the light **Dausol**, the dead's sun. The kingdom under it calls it **the Guest**, and puts out chairs.

**The land.** The two halves are built the way the domain line suggests. The sunward half (the maps say **Twin Suns**) is harvest coast: grain, vine-terraces, and the ferry-quays at **Solkai**, where the standard Midarra crossing lands the north-south trade of half a continent (see `../../cosmology.md`, the Twin Suns ↔ Rika Tikur / Dreaming Cape crossing). The shadow half (**Bikitsa**, the twin-shadow) rises west into cooler hills around its lake: cellar-country, oven-country, the half where things are finished. Offshore east, in its own grey water, the Soul Tree island stands: the door through which souls pass to the Postlife, kept by the **Voroir Daua** under the oldest charter that order holds (see `../../factions.md`).

**One people.** Fiercely one. A visitor who talks of "the Brauogi side" is corrected by everyone from the harbourmaster to the child selling plums, kindly, the way you correct a person who has misread a family. Three centuries of the Service have cross-wed the halves so thoroughly that a Tvisolan is from both the way a person is from both parents; which side of the line you were born on decides only which name you first learned for the light. Deal with either half and you will find you have dealt with the kingdom.

## The year gives twice

The bright year is an ordinary golden coast: first harvest, the quays loading grain southward. At midsummer the kingdom sows again, a sowing every other country on Talan would call wasted, for the aftercrop will not ripen on Solyra's light alone. When the Guest rises, the second growing begins, and Guestide ends with the strangest harvest on the continent: grain and grapes brought in by the light of two suns, the sheaves throwing double shadows across the stubble.

**Erfivin and the Waking Bread.** The dark aftercrop vine is grown on the sunward terraces and pressed at the season's end; the young wine then crosses the line, every barrel of it, to age in Bikitsa's lake-cooled cellars, because the making needs the shadow half's dark the way the growing needs the second light. The result is **Erfivin** (Old Norse *erfi*, the funeral-feast, + *vín*, wine), the funeral wine, the cup poured at wakes from Merkavar to Vindboorg; custom on every coast reserves it for the dead's table, so a small corner kingdom is quietly present at every death on Talan. The aftercrop grain goes the same road: harvested sunward, baked in the shade-half oven-town of **Gaulabe** over the **Guest-Fire**, flame taken from Dausol's first rising with mirror and tinder and let die when the Guest leaves. Kept fires remember; this fire is let go on purpose (the deliberate inverse of Egulon's ever-fire Betisu). The loaf is the **Waking Bread**, set out at wakes in two portions: the named portion, spoken over with the dead's name so the name is kept, and the unnamed portion, for the forgotten dead, no name asked. The kingdom does at every table what Sarrum does at Lurrath with the scroll of the unremembered and what Araphel does on the Lantern Stairs, and it does both at once, which is the whole country in one custom.

**Guestide.** The dead's season is the generous season, and the kingdom keeps it warm: harvest-tables in the streets, an empty chair at every table for the Guest, music that outsiders expect to be dirges and is not. The ferries run heavy those weeks, and some passengers are booked one way: the dying of a dozen kingdoms, come to end under the light they will cross into, hosted in the hospice houses by the young of the Last Service. A Guestide death is held here to be the gentlest there is, and the kingdom takes the hosting of it as its honour and its trade at once.

## The Stywards

Tvisol is governed by the young, on the kingdom's own logic: in a country whose business is death, choices belong to those who must live with them longest.

**The office.** Rule is held by **the Stywards** (English *steward*, spelled back toward its root *stiward*, "keeper of the house"): a wedded or betrothed pair, one born on each half, crowned in their mid-twenties and serving a fixed term of ten years. Their court keeps two seats and crosses with the light: the bright year at Solkai, Guestide on the shadow half.

**The Service.** Eligibility is earned, never bought, through **the Service**, in three degrees. **The Crossing**: a season worked on the other half (sun-half children cellaring and baking in the shade, shade-half children on the fields and quays), which most of the kingdom's children do; it is half rite-of-passage, half how the one-people weave is rewoven each generation. **The House-Year**: a live-in year across the line, which qualifies the lesser offices (reeve, quay-master, keeper of the record). **The Last Service**: the Guestide years with the funerals, the hospices, and the dying, which a minority complete and which alone makes a couple eligible. All of it is public. The Service-years are famously where the matches form; the grandmothers say the kingdom's weddings are planned by the dead.

**The choosing.** Pairs declare themselves; the choosing belongs to **the Old Pairs**, the living former Stywards, four to six couples at any time, the only people who know what the office costs and who have watched every candidate carry grief well or badly since the Crossing. The Old Pairs hold voice, memory, and the choosing; command they set down at thirty-five and never take up again. Should a Styward die in office, the surviving partner may complete the term alone, or step down to an early choosing.

**The setting-down.** A reign ends at its tenth Guestide, setting-down and crowning in one ceremony under the Guest, and abdication is a rite rather than a defeat: the first rehearsal of the release everyone here eventually makes. The reign's works are cut into the record forever, and the ex-Stywards live on for half a century as ordinary subjects of the laws they made, farming, ferrying, pouring wine at the festival, which is an accountability no other constitution on Talan can offer. Foreign courts learn one thing quickly: sign a thirty-year treaty with Tvisol and the ruler who signed it will personally be alive, and a private citizen, to hold you to year thirty.

**The blame-burning.** The record keeps every deed, but an ex-Styward who can stand at the Guest-Fire and give the record their own whole account of a failure may burn the blame with the season; after that the reign may be cited and the ruler may not be cursed. Some do it the first Guestide after their term. Some carry it twenty years first. One or two never manage it, and everyone knows who.

**Founding.** The constitution is the Dark Era's handwriting. The corruption-waves took the coast's grown generation, and the children who survived rebuilt the kingdom; when order returned and the greybeards grew back, the coast decided the young had done it better, and kept what the catastrophe wrote. The Stywards' line of reigns runs unbroken from the early Adventurer Era (~2140s MR); the present pair is roughly the fortieth, and the record reckons the kingdom's history in reigns.

## The peoples

Tvisol anchors no single people; it collects them, the way ports and festivals do. Minotaur terrace-families whose patience turned from stone to vines; Kholo ferry-clans who keep the crossing the way their kin keep herds; Dragonet cellar-wrights entirely at home with time spent in the dark; Fetchling hospice-keepers whom the dying find easy company, being unasked; Goloma saldos hired every Guestide for the night-harvest, many eyes in the low light. And Tvisol is one of Talan's two great sources of **Duskwalkers**, born where the door runs close, and the only place where such a birth is entirely ordinary: a Duskwalker baby here is a child the door smiled on, and the neighbours bring bread. Many take to the Last Service in time; as many grow up to be vintners, reeves, and ferry-masters, which the kingdom considers exactly as it should be. (See `../../ancestries.md`, Duskwalker.)

## Faith

The kingdom keeps both its gods the way a house keeps both grandmothers, and no one in it finds that strange: Sarrum's chapels keep the record and the names, Araphel's keep the unasked and the letting-go, and the year needs both. **Epairima**'s funerary folk-cult is stronger here than anywhere on Talan; her lamp is what most Tvisolans quietly believe the Guest to be (see `../../gods.md`, *Epairima*). **Solyra**'s clergy, whose goddess is never paired and never doubled, have insisted for centuries that the second light is no sun at all, and the kingdom agrees with them cheerfully: Solyra is the sun; the Guest is the Guest. The **Voroir Daua** keep their island charter, oldest of their order, attend the festival every year, and watch the Guestide ferries with an attention the kingdom has learned to read.

## The pressure of the open era

Since the dungeons stirred in 2524 MR the continent has held more wakes than any decade in living memory, and the dead's demand runs against a fixed supply: the aftercrop grows only under the Guest, and no acre of it can be hurried. Prices have tripled, and counterfeit Erfivin has appeared in foreign ports, ordinary wine sold black to grieving families, which the kingdom takes as the one insult it will spend treaty and coin to hunt; the present Stywards seal every true barrel and have made fraud on the dead a matter between crowns. And the age has begun sending the island other visitors: powerful strangers whose interest in the door is not grief. The Voroir Daua have asked the kingdom, for the first time in the charter's long memory, to help them look at Guestide's pilgrims twice, and the country whose festival is an open table is deciding, carefully, what to do about the request.

**◈ Popular Belief: the second shadow.** During Guestide everyone in Tvisol casts two shadows, and everyone in Tvisol will tell you what the second one is: your dead, walking with you. Widows note which side it keeps. Children born in the season are said to have a friendly shadow all their lives. And the old belief beneath it: the shadow the Guest throws is the shape the dead see of you, so a Tvisolan minds how they stand in the second light, because someone is looking.

**⚿ GM Secret: the door's light.** Dausol is exactly what the kingdom's folk-faith almost knows. The Soul Tree is a standing passage between the Life Layer and the Postlife, and around it the veil is worn thin; when Dauria's moon-cycle channels run full, the thinning opens wide enough that the light of the other side stands visible in the living sky. What shines through is the **Duskmire**, the twilight sea where the stars are the lights of distant souls, and behind it the lamp of **Dauria** itself, the two indistinguishable at this distance: the glow of the dead going home, read by the living as a warm low sun (see `../../cosmology.md`, *Planar Structure*). The season keys to the moons, which the Starwrights of Izarelai could confirm from their lake if anyone thought to ask them. The aftercrop ripens under it because the light is thick with the Wellspring's returning charge, life answering the light of life going home; the vines are fed, in the plainest sense, by the crossing of souls, and the continent drinks that vintage at its funerals without knowing how exactly right the custom is. The door has a second implication the Voroir Daua's founders understood perfectly: the Duskmire is one of the two veils where **shards of divinity** wash up (see `../../cosmology.md`, *The Mortal Ascent Ladder*), and the Soul Tree is a natural stair toward it that does not require dying. The order's oldest charter has never primarily been about the island's pilgrims. It is the watch on the one door through which a sufficiently learned mortal could walk toward the Ascent Ladder's raw material and come back; the door-seekers now arriving under Guestide's cover suggest that somewhere, someone has worked this out again.
