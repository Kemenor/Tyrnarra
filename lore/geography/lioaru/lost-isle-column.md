**Golivander Tessek, *A Traveller's Guide to Tyrnarra*:**

> *⟨ISLE⟩: two days by saltkeel; the three essences are sold at the ⟨PORT⟩ quay, where the visitor's business begins and ends; the nearest crater town lies a day's road inland, and it did not rain.*

**Vyrenna Tessek, *One Line Further*, the Talan years:**

**The quay.** My grand-uncle stayed one day and it did not rain, and he wrote that down, because he is honest. I read the line twice on the crossing and decided the only thing to do with it was to stay until it did.

The quay at ⟨PORT⟩ is like every quay I have stood on, loud and crowded and smelling of tar, until you notice that nobody on it looks at the sea. They look inland. Three mountains stand behind the town, far off and blue, each with its own cap of cloud, and the buyers on the quay watch the caps the way farmers watch a sky. When one darkens, the chalk prices on the warehouse boards change before anyone could possibly know anything. I asked which road rains most. Everyone said gold, the way you would tell a child which way is down.

**The first gold.** I lodged with a family in ⟨GOLD TOWN⟩: a mother who keeps accounts, a grandmother who keeps everyone, and a boy of nine who keeps a bowl by the door the way other boys keep a ball.

On my third afternoon a bell rang from the rim and the street emptied upward. I mean that: everyone went onto the roofs. The roofs here all slope to one gutter, and the gutters slope to the street, and the street is cut with channels like a carved board. By the time I had found the stairs, the rain was falling and the whole town was standing in it with boards and brooms, steering water.

It looks like ordinary rain until it lands. Then every runnel turns the colour of late honey and goes down the street toward the square, where the pans are, and the town walks beside it, turning it with the boards at every corner so none of it gets away. The boy ran ahead with his bowl, chasing the thin streams that jumped the channels, and brought each one back like a lost goat.

They work until the light goes, because at sundown it is over. I tried to keep a puddle. I scooped a handful from a gap in the stones where no board could reach, and held it, and watched it while the sun went down, and somewhere in the last red minute it was water in my hands, and then it was only cold. The grandmother saw me standing there with wet palms and laughed until she had to sit down on the step.

By that evening I knew the name of everyone on the street, which my readers will recognise as pure Vorkat and my editor as another week's delay.

**Gold again.** It rained gold again on the sixth day, and the ninth, and the twelfth. A guest learns quickly that the work is the same every time and nobody minds.

What I did not expect was everything else that happens before dark. On the ninth day a neighbour came to the door at dusk, still wet from the roofs, to tell the mother she had kept his board two rains running and he wanted it back or he wanted it settled. They had it out on the step, briskly, and settled it, and he stayed for supper. The mother told me afterwards that a quarrel kept overnight goes bad like milk. I had seen three such doorsteps by then, and I believe her.

She keeps her accounts in gold ink. I had heard it glows. It does not. In the evenings she writes by a lamp so low I would not read by it at home, and the figures stand up off the page as if someone had underlined them all, and she needs the lamp all the same. When I caught a cold, she put a drop in my tea and told me it would cure me. It helped, a little. She knows it helps a little; she says *cure* anyway, the way mothers do.

**Starlight.** I went up the second road on the fourteenth day, because the boy told me starlight was the best and the mother told me it was for mages. Both are true. It rains less there, and the town is quieter and richer, and its houses are larger. I waited six days. When it came it looked like nothing at all in the gutter, plain wet stone, until you crouched and saw that every drop had a cold point of light in it, as if the street had been strewn with the night before night came. I did not try to keep any. I had learned.

**Silver, at last.** The third road climbs to the crater on the island's far side, and from its rim on a clear night the west keeps a pale edge that never quite goes out. They call it ⟨the far light⟩. The Cloud Sea is out there, much too far to see; you see its light.

Silver rains least, and I was told so by everyone, so I took a room and stayed. It rained on my ninth day there. The rain was so thin I could not see the colour in it, only a sheen on the channels, like breath on a mirror. The town drew it into the pans anyway, every roof and every street, until sundown. Then they began on the stills, and worked through the night, and in the morning the master of the stills showed me what the whole town had caught. She scraped it from the bottom of the pan into a vial no longer than my finger, grey-white, and held it to the window, and said it was a good rain.

I left ⟨ISLE⟩ on a Tellenn morning, with the boy's bowl in my luggage; he says he will want it back, and I am to bring it myself. I am writing this on the saltkeel, in his mother's gold ink, by a lamp I had to borrow. Maren, if the page is easy to read, that is the ink.
