**Voice block.** **Vyrenna Tessek** · Tengu of clan Tessek, of Haizetsua, in the Talan years of *One Line Further* (2527 MR), travelling alone, before Wren. Stays twelve days where her grand-uncle saw one city; homesick for the sky in a city without one; the music peeve turned to praise, for a city the wind plays. Wind-image: Skarrenn (homesickness).

**Golivander Tessek, *A Traveller's Guide to Tyrnarra*:**

> *The Twin Cities: two of them, one on the water and one above it, and both move. Ask on any Midarra quay where they were last month and sail somewhere near it; the stillship masters who know will charge for the knowing, and the ones who charge most know least. Bring coin in small pieces and a purse you would not miss. The taverns never close, there being no hour at which they could sensibly be said to. I saw the city on the water. The other was out.*
>
> *The tea is stewed.*

**Vyrenna Tessek, *One Line Further*, the Talan years:**

**Finding it.** My grand-uncle saw one of the two cities and reported, with perfect honesty, that the other was out. I have never once in my life been able to leave a thing at *out*, so I took passage on a grain stillship whose master swore she knew where the pirates were this month, and charged me for it, and we spent nine days proving her wrong.

On the tenth morning the lookout called. I went up and saw what I took for an island with a storm sitting on it. It was ships. Hulls stacked on hulls, a whole fleet piled into a mountain, black and brown and every colour paint has ever been, with masts still standing out of it at every angle like a forest after a gale. Round its foot sailed a ring of ships that were still ships, under canvas, keeping pace with the mountain, and the mountain was moving. On its very top stood a handful of spires, each one a tall ship stood on its end, each flying its own flag. Our master took her hat off when she saw the flags. She told me later it was habit.

**Under the hulls.** You walk in along a gangway, and the gangway widens, and doors open on both sides, and somewhere in the first hundred paces it has become a street and the sky has gone. The decks close over you. Ships are hoisted onto ships here. The tavern by the landing is four hulls tall, its top deck run across on beams to the gambling hall next door, and on top of the whole tavern sits a fifth ship with lace at its portholes, where the owner lives. Every gangway is a hallway, every hallway a promenade, and the promenades are lined with shops lit by lanterns, because the lanterns are all the light there is. The sun stays outside and the moon with it, and the city has decided that suits it fine. The baker had bread out at what my body insisted was three in the morning, and the queue was as long as a queue at home at breakfast.

I had my crest trimmed by a barber on the third deck who hummed the whole way through, and he laid the razor along the feathers at my neck as gently as a mother's hand. I told him he had a light touch. He said he had two.

**The vote.** Every street in this city was once a ship, and they still run them that way. On my fourth day, on a promenade called the [Lower Spar], I watched its people vote out their captain over a leaking roof. Forty-one hands went up. He looked at them for a while, then bought them all a drink, and the new captain drank his and went to look at the roof. Nobody I asked found it remarkable. Everyone who lives here is crew, they told me: the baker, the barber, the woman who sells hot nuts at the corner of the Spar. A captain who forgets it gets reminded, by hands or otherwise. The Lords in their spires too. The Lords most of all.

**The bilges.** Below the lowest street the city goes on downward. The weight has pushed the oldest hulls under, some flooded and some dry, and the dry ones are workshops and storerooms with the sea on the far side of every plank. I went down a ladder into one and found a window in its side, thick old glass with the water pressed green against it, and a face on the other side looking in at me, scaled at the temples, hair lifting in the water like smoke. She raised a hand. I raised mine. What the air-breathers stop using, the water-folk move into.

**The sky comes home.** I had begun to miss the sky the way I miss my mother: badly, and in my stomach. I am a child of the wind, and Skarrenn found me on the fifth day in a city with no wind in it at all, and I sat on a coil of rope and sulked like a fledgling. The hot-nut woman told me to wait. "The other half's out," she said. "Comes back when it comes back."

It came back on the eighth day. A shout went down the gangways and the whole city climbed. I climbed with it, up stairs that had been ladders and ladders that had been masts, onto the highest open deck I could find. Out in the west, low over the water, there were four islands in the air.

They came on slowly. Dark rock underneath, bearded with roots, the edge of a city glinting along the top, and under each one a cluster of airships on long lines, straining, like gulls carrying something too heavy for them and too precious to drop. As they came over the city they put out the evening, and the deck I stood on went cool and blue. Then it rained. Out of a clear sky it rained on the top of the city: cloud-water shaken off the islands as they settled, cold and fine. And I watched a whole city that had not seen the sky in weeks turn its face up into it with its eyes closed.

By midnight the lines were down. Baskets dropped from the islands, gangways swung across, and the two markets poured into each other like two rivers meeting: the lantern-pale folk from below and the sun-brown folk from above, every one of them buying something they could not get at home and shouting about the price.

**Up.** I went up in a basket, which I recommend to no one.

At the top was the brightest place I have ever stood. The sky city is all open: wide squares of stone, a table and chairs out in front of every door, awnings snapping, and hammocks slung everywhere, between posts, between houses, out over the very edge, with people asleep in them above nothing at all. Every wall has windows, and half the windows are only holes, cut where the wind comes through. The wind comes through all of them, and the whole city hums.

I am always honest with you about music, so here it is. I have heard famous halls play the same piece a thousand nights and mean it once. Nobody plays this city; the wind does, and the wind has never pretended in its life. It sang the way Haizetsua sings. I stood in a square with a cup of something hot going cold in my hands and cried, and a sky pirate with a ring through her eyebrow sat down beside me and let me.

She had been in the clouds that morning. Her crew dive their ship into them on purpose, for the feel of it: in blind and white, out wet to the skin, every droplet cold as a coin. She showed me her arms, still beaded, and laughed at my face.

When the stars came out, the sky city stayed up too. It twinkles. Lamps along every edge, voices carried off on the wind, and laughter coming down out of the dark onto the decks below, where the sea-folk lay on their backs to listen.

**The last day.** They parted on the twelfth day. The lines came up, the baskets came up, the islands leaned into the wind and went east together, and the stacked city lit its lanterns and went about its business. I asked the hot-nut woman whether they would come back. She looked at me as if I had asked about the tide.

On the quay I asked an old pirate which city was the real one. He said both. Then he said Frae City was free because a god told it so, and the Twin Cities were free because they said so themselves, every morning, and could stop saying it any time they liked. "That's the difference," he said. "Write that down."
