The Low Span
The fighting pit · the bridge district
A fight, a crowd, and money changing hands by lamplight. What's above the sand is no secret. What's below it, nobody who knows is saying.
Where
The southern bridge
The Ring
30-foot sand circle

The Establishment

The Low Span started as a fighting ring in the southernmost bridge's undercroft about forty years ago: two dockworkers settling a disagreement in front of an audience that was supposed to be three people and became thirty. Someone started taking bets. Someone else started charging admission. By the time anyone thought to ask who owned it, ownership had become a complicated question answered by three different names on three different documents.

The ground-floor ring is entirely above board: agreed rules, consenting fighters, betting conducted openly and taxed, in theory, by the bridge district's self-appointed revenue collector. It is loud, warm, and one of the few places in Millhaven where the Cape side and the Stillside stand at the same rail and shout for the same fighter.

As You Come In

You smell it before you find it: sawdust and river damp and the particular noise a crowd makes when it has put money on something. The entrance is a door set into the bridge's south face, unmarked but for a small brass compass-rose scratched into the wood at eye level. Inside, the ceiling is the underside of the bridge itself, raw timber and iron bolts, and the floor drops away in tiers toward a sand circle thirty feet across, lit by oil lamps on chains that sway with the river. Nobody looks at you when you walk in. In a place like this, not being looked at is a form of welcome.

Tonight's Card

The ring runs three to five bouts a night, dusk to midnight, with a late card on Fridays that goes until the lamps run low. Fighters contract per bout and take a cut of the betting pool. The Low Span provides the sand, the lamps, and the crowd, and offers no opinions on outcomes.

The Regulars

Bookkeeper · ground floor
Marta
Halfling, fifties

She has run the Low Span's books longer than most of the crowd has been coming. She works from a stool near the entrance with a leather satchel of coin and will quote you the odds on anything, including bouts not currently on the card, if you know to ask. She has a quiet sense of who in town has a gambling problem and adjusts their credit without being asked.

Fighter · headliner regular
The Sable Woman
No name given

She fights under no name; the card has just said "The Sable Woman" for three years. Exceptional, efficient, and winning in a way that suggests she could end her bouts earlier than she does and chooses not to. Watch her closely and you'll catch it: she holds back, the way someone might who once fought where losing was fatal and has decided this ring is exactly as much risk as she wants.

Barkeeper
Cord
Human, large and quiet

He tends bar and has, in his years here, heard approximately everything the bridge district has to say to itself. He has never repeated any of it. He serves anyone without comment and will not serve twice anyone who has caused a problem. Earn his read of you as neither stupid nor dangerous, and he will, once, answer a single direct question with one accurate sentence and no more.

Bridge Council · merchant lord
Sable Rei
Always at the rail

Present most nights, standing at the second-tier rail like anyone else who paid to get in, in the same coat and a different face. Bets on whoever is more interesting regardless of the odds, collects without counting, and is in quiet conversation with someone between every bout if you watch closely enough to notice. If you keep coming back and handle yourselves well, Sable Rei may one night drift to the rail beside you, and that is its own kind of invitation.

Everyone in Millhaven knows the real betting is downstairs, and everyone is a little vague on how they know. The story goes that under the public ring there is a second floor, reached by a trapdoor behind the bar, where the rules are fewer and the stakes higher and the bridge district's actual business gets done across card tables; and under that, a third room that is not a room at all but a platform out over the river, where what is decided is decided where the current takes the sound away. Whether any of this is so, nobody who has seen it is saying, and the people who would know seem to prefer the rumour to the truth, because a rumour you can deny.
