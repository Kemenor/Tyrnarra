The Crossing

Millhaven grew the way river-crossings grow: a mill where the water narrowed before its last run to the coast, then a waystation, then a market, then a second market for the boatmen the first one drew, and then someone roofed a tavern over the middle bridge and discovered they were printing coin. The Smáa is the border river itself, running down to part the dreaming Cape on the west from steadfast Itsasalda on the east. A town that straddles the Smáa straddles the line between the two, and Millhaven has spent its whole existence being two places that share a name and a set of bridges.

What You Need to Know Walking In

Ask where someone is from in Millhaven and they answer with a bank, never the town. The two shores keep two tempers, and the bridges between them keep a third. Nobody is wholly in charge: the Cape side answers, in the end, to Moonwatch; the Stillside to Bellhalt; and the bridges in the middle answer to no one at all, which is exactly why so much business gets done on them.

The Three Faces

West bank · the Cape side
The Cape Side

Weathered wood and salt-stone, a fishing fleet and the boats that would rather their cargo went unlisted, dock-inns that trade on a good night's sleep. The Cape's drift bleeds this far east and thins as it comes, heaviest in the oldest lanes the locals call the Drift Quarter.

The middle · no bank's ground
The Bridges

Three bridges never built to carry this many buildings, shops and stairs cantilevered over the water and spilling onto pilings. A floating quarter that pays no bank's tax, because each bank's claim cancels the other's over the middle. The Wayward Compass and the Low Span both sit here.

East bank · the Stillside
The Stillside

Wider streets, taller granaries, a proper market square, and the tide-watching temper of a people whose oldest saying is the king will go; the dock will not. It takes its name from a plain fact: cross the Smáa and the drift dies, the world holds still, and the clocks agree again.

The Drift's Edge

The Dreaming Cape's drift, the waking mind half-touched by dream, reaches Millhaven thinned by the distance from the Cape's heart. Here it is a fractional gap between a sound and its source; a fisherman sure he has hauled the same net twice; clocks on the Cape side that never quite agree with the clocks on the Stillside. It pools strongest in the oldest Cape-side lanes, which locals avoid after dark, not for danger but for the disorientation, and it stops dead at the river.

What causes it is genuinely disputed. Cape-side doctrine names it a blessing of the twin moons spilling east; Stillside folk name it sea-fog, or forge-smoke, or simply the Cape being the Cape. Nobody agrees, and the town has long since stopped expecting them to.

The Cape-side taverns trade on the drift as readily as they trade on fish-stew, and the tales thicken with the hour. They say a night in the Drift Quarter shows you the next morning before you live it; that two fishers once woke on the same Tuesday twice, and one found a note in his own hand he had no memory of writing; that a child born in the oldest lanes answers a question a breath before it is asked and grows out of it by ten. The Stillside hears all this with the patience of people whose own clocks keep fine time, and says the Cape would sell you the fog if it could bottle it. What everyone agrees on, both banks, is that you do not make firm plans in the Drift Quarter past noon.

The Merchant Council

Millhaven governs its own crossing, and has for centuries, because neither power on the banks has ever fully closed its hand on it. The two banks are bound to each other by the bridges and the trade across them, so in every matter that is actually Millhaven's, the banks answer to each other as much as to their regions, and the town runs on the muddle.

To work it the town keeps a Merchant Council of nineteen seats: eight to the Cape side, eight to the Stillside, three to the Bridge merchants, and ten votes to pass anything. Locked at eight apiece, neither bank carries a thing alone, so every contested ruling turns on the three Bridge seats, and the Bridge merchants love it. The Wayward Compass's Guildmaster, Aldric Fenn, holds one of those three seats and votes the Guild's neutrality, which infuriates both banks equally.

Faith · Three Parts, Three Gods

the Cape side
Bikiargi

Bikiargi of the twin moons holds the Cape side, in her paired aspect, acknowledged at the dock-inns and strongest in the Drift Quarter. Hers is the bank where the dream comes closest to the waking street.

the Stillside
Jianna

Jianna of Commerce, the domain's own goddess, holds the Stillside: the closed hand of the running, kept contract, the held deal blessed in its honouring. The faith of a bank that prizes the deal that does not break the way it prizes the dock that does not move.

the bridges
Shuun

Shuun of the water holds the bridges, which is only fitting, for the bridge-folk live over the river and on it. The Smáa is Shuun's road through the town, and the fishers and barge-captains of both banks toss a token to the current when they retire a route.
