Breidey
Floating Isles · the Broad Isle
Honest work for honest money, and the evening is guaranteed.
✦

At a Glance

Etymology
Icelandic breið (broad) + ey (island): "the broad isle." The port town is Breidhofn, "the broad harbour." Demonym: Breideyer.
Position
East of Uravel, the largest island of the drifting archipelago; a League island, on the Lists, fielding more keels than the next five islands together and holding one vote for it.
The Island
The one thing a drifting archipelago never has enough of is room, and Breidey has room left over: fields run down to slipways, slipways to workshop-quarters, workshop-quarters to quays. Everything the Isles must choose between, Breidey simply does.
Character
The port that never stops, until Fyrsta sings.
Bargain-StateHelmworksThe Rolls
Peoples
An open island: half the population's parents were born somewhere else, and the saying runs that the difference between a Breideyer and a Keelborn is only how fast their home moves. Anyone willing to put in an honest day finds a home.
Faith
Shuun's, kept the working way: the launch blessed, the harvest thanked, and the covenant honored, which a Breideyer will tell you is the same thing said three times.
Rule
Houses and the Rolls, bound by the Settlement, renegotiated every fourth year at the Long Table; the Chair of the Settlement speaks for the island. Honest work for honest money, binding in both directions.

The Port That Never Stops

You step off the boat, and before you have taken a full breath you hear a shout and step aside: three barrels roll past you onto the boat you just left. Breidhofn at working hours is total motion, loading, unloading, counting, weighing, running calculations, everyone working, and the restlessness of it is the first thing every visitor writes home about. Your contact finds you with a shout: well-dressed, sun-baked, and when he grabs your forearm and shakes it, the way all Breideyers greet, the arm is trained and strong. It has rolled barrels; it worked its way up; it still rolls one now and then when the day demands, and it would be an insult to suggest otherwise.

He shows you the workshop, the workers, and the product before the office, because that is the honest order; and when you reach consensus, price, prototype delivery, and the assay-terms all settled before supper, he grabs your arm again and you shake on it, and the shake is the contract. Then he invites you to dinner at his house, and would have if the deal had died on the table. The Breidey arm seals bargains and greets strangers with the same gesture, and the island means something by that.

Fyrsta Sings

At day's end an instrument sounds across the whole island, and Breidey stops. The instrument is the Fyrsta: the first hull Breidey ever launched, retired into music instead of salvage, her hull strung as a resonating body the size of a house and played from within, a hullharp, the only one of her scale on Talan. Her song carries over water and workshop alike, and at the first phrase every tool on the island goes down. Top buttons open. One long exhale. The restlessness everyone carried all day was never worry; it was appetite for an evening that is guaranteed, and within a quarter hour the busiest place on the inner sea is the most relaxed: friends at tables, dockhands laughing in the water where the barrels rolled at noon. The phrase for the day's end, island-wide, is "Fyrsta sings." Visitors are told to finish their business before she does, and the ones who mistake the stop for laziness are invited, gently, to come watch the morning start.

Honest Work for Honest Money

That sentence is Breidey's whole constitution, and it binds in both directions. The island is owned by its nobility, plain and acknowledged: Houses, no king, titles as real as their ledgers. And the Houses are bound to the covenant like everyone else. A House does its due diligence, puts in the effort, meets its payroll, and is honored for it; a House that stops working finds its workshops without workers, its boats full of barrels nobody will unload, and its own cook serving bread and water at the long table, courteously, until the lesson lands. A House that hemorrhages money finds there is always a merchant ready to buy the title and the land and restructure, and a restructuring is what Breidey calls it, the polite word and the brutal one being deliberately the same.

The Open Island

Half the population's parents were born somewhere else; folk come to work and find a home. The only test is the day's labor, and the door has never narrowed.

The Rolls

The all-trades union, which is everyone. A newcomer who works an honest day is on the Rolls by that evening: you roll barrels, you are on the Rolls, and no Breideyer has ever needed the pun explained.

The Closed Counter

The sanction on the unrolled: no bread from any baker, no room from any landlord, no passage from any ferryman, no exceptions and no hard feelings, until an honest day's work opens every counter at once.

The Settlement

Each House governs its own holdings outright, wards, docks, fields, and works alike. What binds the island whole is the Settlement: the master-agreement between the two tables, the Houses and the Rolls, renegotiated every fourth year at the Long Table and signed with the largest arm-shake on the Isles. The Settlement fixes wages, hours, dues, walkout rules, the joint fund for roads and quays, and the song's hour, for the daily stop is a clause of the Settlement made audible, which is why nobody mistakes the rest for a favor. Both tables elect the Chair of the Settlement for the cycle: the Chair receives envoys, signs treaties, and instructs Breidey's voice at the Raft.

The founding is told with equal pride on both sides of the table: the Year the Barrels Sat, the great walkout of the founding generations, when the boats filled and nothing moved and the proudest Houses learned what an island full of workers thinks; it ended with nobles rolling barrels beside their crews and the first Settlement signed. The Long Table has been the island's recurring political fever ever since, and a failed Settlement, which has happened, stops the machine that awes the inner sea entirely.

The Helmworks

Space gave Breidey workshops; the drifting sea gave the workshops their problem, for a sea whose geography is weather demands instruments that do not lie; and generations of solving it made Breidey's helmworks the quiet standard of the Midarra. Bearing-keepers, drift-clocks, the fine Magitech of knowing where you are on a moving sea: made by the helmwrights in small numbers to exacting patterns, every piece Breidhofn-sealed, every serious captain on the inner sea owning at least one and trusting no substitute. The Order of Steam stamps at a scale Breidey has never envied; Breidey makes the pieces that go where scale cannot: at the helm, in the hand, right when the fog closes. Captains make the pilgrimage to Breidhofn to buy at the workshop, negotiate the assay-terms like everyone else, and seal it with the arm.

✦

The Investment

Breidey fielded most of the League's fighting keels in 2524, and fields most of them still, and any Breideyer will correct the mainland telling of this with mild impatience: it was never a sacrifice. It is an investment. Breidey keels broke the corruption wave at sea; Breidey timber went down in the sealing of the drowned gate; Breidey books carry all of it, calmly, in the asset column. The island that thinks in ledgers by reflex looked at the League and took a long position generations ago, and holds it with complete confidence and one growing uncertainty, which is not whether the position pays but when. The keel-franchise argument and the Standing Watch campaign are, in Breidey's own accounting, simply the dividend coming due: the Watch would need hulls, patterns, yards, and a leader, and Breidey has all four. The only thing the family boards argue about, at the Long Table and after Fyrsta sings, is the payout horizon, and the arguments are getting shorter.

Islanders swear the old hull chooses her moment: that she sings a little early on days a storm is coming in, and a little late on launch days, as if she wants to watch the new hull down the slip before she calls the island home. The players insist they keep the hour to the minute, every day, per the Settlement. The island has heard the players' version, thanked them for it, and continues to believe its own.

⚿   Inventory

On three covered slips above Breidhofn stand three hulls that no one ordered. They are built to patrol patterns, provisioned for standing crews, and entered in the House ledgers as inventory; the yard-crews who work them are on quiet wages and quieter contracts. Breidey is building the Standing Watch before the Raft has voted for it, because that is what you do when you are certain of a position: you take delivery early.

If the vote passes, Breidey delivers a working Watch in months instead of years, and whoever delivers the Watch leads it. If the vote keeps failing, the island is holding three warship-pattern hulls it must eventually sell, and every buyer on the inner sea that isn't the League is a problem the family boards have agreed, so far, to leave off the agenda. The Chair knows. The Rolls' senior stewards know, because their members are building them. The Raft does not.
