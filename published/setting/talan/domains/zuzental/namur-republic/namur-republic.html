The Namur Republic
Zuzental Sub-Region · The Sworn Republic · The Dictator by Oath
A republic that keeps its word, including the one that made it a dictatorship.
✦

At a Glance

Etymology
Namur is the ground's name, older than the Republic, and the Republic took it; the capital is Portoferma. Demonym: Namurese.
Position
South-western Zuzental, a wedge of steppe and coast on the Midarra. The Legea Empire lies north, and Portoferma sits at the mouth of Legea's river, under the western flank of the Orratzak; a thin strip of Namur runs up the coast west of the needles. The Order of Law lies east beyond a knot of mountains; the great river runs the southern border, with Atarialda (Lautara) across it.
Terrain
Dry steppe and sandy grass over most of it, a forest in the middle, hills in the south-west with the old mine-workings in them, and the mountains on the Order of Law line. The Sickwell, the Blight-Seer's gate, broke open in the old southern workings in 2524.
Character
A republic that keeps its word
The Sworn RepublicThe Oath-DayThe Long WalkThe SickwellYear Eight
Peoples
About 1.8 million. Every ancestry of Zuzental and the southern network lives here, and Hobgoblins most, at their fullest expression on Talan.
Tongue
Talanese; the country's names are in its own register.
Faith
Forseti, as everywhere in Zuzental: her Lawspeakers witness the citizens' oaths and the Oath-Day.
Rule
The Senate, elected district by district on oaths its senators write themselves, with a First Senator presiding; and above it, when the Republic swears one, a Dictator, re-sworn every year on the Oath-Day until the danger is past. The present Dictator, Aurelio Brandin detto Saldo, has been sworn since 2524.
Founded
Late in the Golden Era, as a chartered free city of the Empire.
The Capital
Portoferma

The Sworn Republic

"The hall carries a portrait of every senator, which in itself should not be a notable sight for a man of the Kingdom, and neither should the seriousness captured in them. It is a sight because below each portrait, every name is accompanied by an oath: one the senator wrote themselves, was voted in on, and now carries into the Senate for their people."

Renier Konrad Brask, an armour-factor of Smedbeck, in his journal, 2526

A Namurese becomes a citizen by oath. At coming of age each young Namurese writes their own citizen's oath, what they will be to the Republic and to their town, and swears it before the town with a Lawspeaker of Forseti as witness. The oath is kept in the town's register and read back at their funeral.

The Senate is made the same way. A candidate for the Senate writes an oath of office, what they will do for their district and by when, and the district votes on the oath, not on the candidate. The winning oath hangs under the senator's portrait in the Senate House at Portoferma for the length of the term, six years, and a senator who breaks it loses the seat and the sworn name with it. A Namurese election is argued line by line over the candidates' oaths.

"Perhaps." A people who keep every word they give are slow to give one. A Namurese answers almost everything with perhaps. I will see is courtesy; I will try is a real attempt; I give my word is heard a handful of times in a life, and it holds. Visitors find the Namurese evasive; Namurese merchants lend most readily to the neighbour slowest to promise.

The Senate House at Portoferma is plain stone with the best ironwork in the south on its doors. Its chamber is a bowl sunk into the ground: the people's rows are at the rim, open to anyone, the senators' rows below them, and the First Senator's floor at the bottom.

The fool's oath. Before coming of age a child's oath is sworn by a fool, as the saying goes. It binds lightly, and breaking it costs a forfeit: carry the water for a week, sing on the well-steps, wear the other's cap to market. The young test and tease each other with it, tricking a friend into swearing something absurd and then holding them to it or cheering when they break it. Most get broken; the ones kept out of stubbornness are told about for years. The forfeits grow heavier as a child nears coming of age, so that by sixteen a broken fool's oath already stings. Parents and the town's elders keep watch over one thing: a trick-oath that could hurt someone. You may make a fool of your friend, never a victim, and a child who tricks another into a harmful oath owes the forfeit themselves.

The Office and the Long Walk

"His oath was to draw the corrupted spawn away, and he kept it. His blade is bent, not nicked. That is a charge, not a fight."

Renier Konrad Brask, on the Long Walk, 2526

In a crisis the Republic may swear one citizen as Dictator. The Dictator writes an oath like any senator, what they will hold the Republic safe against and that they will lay the office down when the danger is past, and swears it before the Senate; the Senate swears its obedience back. The Dictator stands above ordinary law until the office lapses. What the Dictator may sign and do is anything the oath requires, and what the oath requires is argued every year.

The Oath-Day. Once a year the Dictator comes down to the First Senator's floor, re-swears the oath, and lays the sword of office on the stone slab at the centre. The senators come down one by one, take the sword up, and speak their oath of obedience to it, or stay in their rows. If fewer than half come down, the office lapses and the sword goes to the rack. If enough come down, the First Senator hands the sword back. The count is carried out of Portoferma the same evening by relay-riders and signal-fires, and the whole country knows the number by morning.

The Long Walk. Upstairs in the Senate House, the gallery before the chamber is hung with the swords of every Dictator the Republic has sworn, each with two dates and the Dictator's oath. The two oldest are Golden-Era work, sworn while Namur was a chartered city of the Empire. Then come the Long Swearing of the Dark Era: the office re-sworn every year for some four and a half centuries of the worst of it, about 1330 to 1780 MR, one Dictator after another, their swords laid down by choice or by death. Vittore Grimal, sworn in 1612, lasted four days: his oath was to draw the spawn off Portoferma's walls, and he kept it.

The Adventurer Era left gaps on the rack, years and decades when the Republic needed no Dictator, and short dictatorships between them: a famine, a plague, a flood. The shortest laid down by choice lasted three weeks.

"Three weeks. The river went down, I went home. You want the red or the white?"

Tommaso Venier detto Buono Posaspada, wine-seller of Portoferma, Dictator in the flood of 2491, to a customer who asked

A Dictator who lays the sword down carries Posaspada after their name for life, and a Dictator who dies in office receives it on the Long Walk plaque. Portoferma has two living Posaspada. One sells wine by the harbour.

The broken sword. Gaspare Loredan was sworn in 2398, when the Legea Empire pressed south, and he won the war. Peace was sworn in 2402. At the next Oath-Day the senators stayed in their rows, the office lapsed, and Loredan kept the sword. The lapse released every Namurese from the oath of obedience, and the Republic took him down in the Senate House itself. His sword hangs broken on the Long Walk, snapped on an anvil a hand above the guard, the blood left on it; his oath is struck through, and his name carries nothing after it. The laying of the sword on the slab dates from his fall: since 2403 the sword leaves the Dictator's hand once a year in front of the whole Senate, and comes back only if the Senate gives it.

The empty hook. At the end of the Long Walk is a hook with no sword on it, a date of 2524, no second date, and the oath of Aurelio Brandin detto Saldo: to hold the Republic safe against the Blight-Seer until the danger is past.

The Gate and Year Eight

"Then he unbuckled his sword and laid it on a stone slab at the centre of the floor and stepped back from it. It is a plain soldier's sword, and it has been working: the edge nicked along its whole length and honed back each time, the grip rewrapped more than once. Two years of the gate."

Renier Konrad Brask, at the Oath-Day of 2526

In 2524 the Nine Dungeons broke open across Talan, and one of them broke up through the old mine-workings in Namur's south-western hills: the Sickwell, the labyrinth of the Blight-Seer, pits of contagion and diseased shrines under a standing miasma. The Senate swore Aurelio Brandin, a garrison captain of the hills, as Dictator within the week. The Republic's militia and its sworn companies called home from abroad fought the first wave through the hill towns and sealed the gate in 2525. They did not clear it. Spawn still come up through the lesser workings, and the survivors of the Seer's cult who crawl out speak in fevered prophecy that sometimes proves true.

The sealing took back part of the hills with it. Some of the old workings were cleared shaft by shaft and are mined again under guard; their ore goes north to the armouries of the Thousand Kingdom. The deeper workings around the Sickwell stay shut, and the garrison holds a ring of walls and watch-fires around them.

The gate rotation. Every citizen owes a season at the gate under the militia oath. Every family in Namur has someone who has stood on the line or is standing there now, and the garrison road south from Portoferma is the best-kept road in the country.

Aurelio fights at the gate himself, and his sword shows it. At the Oath-Day of 2526 every senator came down to the slab. At the Oath-Day of 2532, the eighth, sixty-eight of a hundred and twenty came down.

Folk say the spawn and the miasma from the Sickwell go first for those who have broken their word, so that an oathbreaker on the gate-line endangers everyone beside him. Garrison companies will not stand a man they know to be forsworn at the wall, and more than one soldier has confessed a broken fool's oath from his childhood before his first season, to be safe.

Live Tension · Year Eight

The gate is sealed and still leaks, and the danger is real. Other countries hold a sealed gate without a Dictator: Greenward's cantons, the Baerfrost clans, the order-state of Argia Esfera. The mining families want the deep workings cleared and reopened, which would need a Dictator and an army. The reformers want the sword on the rack while the Republic can still lay it down, and point to the broken sword. The Legea Empire has offered its army for the gate, and Aurelio could sign the offer if the oath requires it; the Senate fears what would march in behind the soldiers, readers and reception-houses in the hill towns. Sixty-eight is eight above the line where the office lapses, and last year's count was seventy-nine.

⚿   The Seer Never Lies

The Blight-Seer is sealed, and it does not need to break out. Its sight seeps up with the miasma, and it shows the people nearest the gate the true rot in others: every real bribe, every real lie, every real betrayal, and never the loyalty, the reasons, or the good beside them. It never lies. It shows only the damning half.

Aurelio, a season at a time on the line for eight years, sees real corruption everywhere in the Republic: a senator's real debts to a Legean factor, a reformer's real family on the far bank. Each year it convinces him honestly that the danger is not past. The senators who serve their own gate seasons see the same thing turned the other way: the real rot in eight years of the Dictator's rule, the purged captain who was guilty of something and innocent of what he was purged for. The growing silences on the Oath-Day are the Seer's work as much as the swearing is.

Either way the count falls, the Seer is served: a Dictator who holds too long, or a Republic that breaks its sword in anger, a whole people judging itself on the prosecution's evidence alone. The fevered prophecies of the cult survivors are the same sight spoken aloud, which is why they sometimes prove true.

Portoferma and Legea

Portoferma stands at the mouth of Legea's river under the western flank of the Orratzak, a port of about sixty thousand, and Debreqal is a short ride up the valley. Since the peace of 2402 the two countries have traded, and much of Legea's river-trade reaches the sea through Portoferma: the barges from Debreqal unload on Namurese quays, pay Namurese customs, and load onto Namurese hulls. The Legean customs-house on the quay flies the Faith's banner beside the Republic's.

The sworn companies. A Namurese soldier's contract-oath holds, and the southern network pays for it: sworn companies of Namurese stand guard on warehouses in Merkavar, escort cargo through Crossroads, and garrison the walls of kingdoms that trust their own soldiers less. In 2524 the Republic called its companies home for the gate, and most have gone back out; their pay keeps the Republic's treasury while the old mines stay shut.

Ore and harness. The retaken mines' ore goes north to the Thousand Kingdom's armouries, and harness comes back south for the gate garrison, so that House Harn of Smedbeck buys from Namur and sells to it in the same season.

What a Namurese Is Called

A Namurese carries a given name and a family name, and at the citizen's oath adds a sworn name: one word from their own oath, chosen by them, carried with detto or detta. Lucia Morosin detta Salda. Senators are known in the chamber by their sworn names. An oathbreaker loses the sworn name, and two names alone mark a Namurese for life. A Dictator who lays the sword down adds Posaspada after the sworn name.

Peoples

Every ancestry of Zuzental lives in Namur, and the Hobgoblins at their fullest expression on Talan: the people for whom the given word is load-bearing, in a country built on it. Elves sit long in the Senate and remember what an oath was sworn for; Humans are most of the reformers and most of the sworn companies; and the Hobgoblin temper is in everything. The Republic's oldest law against itself, that the sword leaves the Dictator's hand every Oath-Day, is Hobgoblin work.

The Journal of Renier Konrad Brask

A Thousand Kingdom armour-factor of Smedbeck, on the business of House Harn, at Portoferma on the Oath-Day of 2526. Published whole.

Today I had my meeting with Senator Matteo Contarin detto Largo. It was scheduled for the late afternoon, so I went to the Senate House at midday, to be early and get the lay of the land, if you will. The entry hall is imposing in its design, but plainer than most great houses in the Kingdom: good stone, no gilding, and the best ironwork on the doors I have seen outside Smedbeck.

The hall carries a portrait of every senator, which in itself should not be a notable sight for a man of the Kingdom, and neither should the seriousness captured in them. It is a sight because below each portrait, every name is accompanied by an oath: one the senator wrote themselves, was voted in on, and now carries into the Senate for their people.

I was excited for what awaited me after climbing the stairs: the Long Walk. I felt it more imposing than the entrance hall; mayhaps that is simply my profession. Each side is lined with swords, each with two dates and an oath, and no portrait.

The swords reach back into the time of the Golden Empire, and all the way to the present. The second is the most pompous of them: a jewelled hilt, Imperial dwarf-work, and barely used, the edge still carrying its first grind. Not much further on they become simple, almost poor in comparison, and every grip is well worn. The Dark Era saw one after another: munition steel, blades reground a finger narrower than they were forged, grips worn through to the tang, swords bent and dented and left as they were laid down, by choice or by death. Some lasted decades. One lasted four days; his oath was to draw the corrupted spawn away, and he kept it. His blade is bent, not nicked. That is a charge, not a fight.

Then you reach the present age and find gaps once more, swords taken up and laid down, until Gaspare Loredan. His sword is broken, a hand above the guard, which no good blade does in a fight; someone broke it on an anvil. The blood on it is still there, dried, and his oath is struck through.

And at the end you find an empty hook, the closing date missing, the oath promising safety against the Blight-Seer.

I walked in on a session already running, and the first thing I did not expect was to be standing higher than everyone in the room. The Senate is built like a theatre sunk into the ground, a great stone bowl, and you enter at the rim. The upper rows belong to the people: anyone may come in and sit, to watch and to witness, and the rows were full. Below them sit the senators, and at the bottom of the bowl, the lowest point in the room, is the First Senator's floor. Everyone who governs Namur does it looking up at the people who let them.

The dictator was on the floor when I came in, re-swearing his oath. I could not hear every word from the rim, but I heard until the danger is past clear enough, and so did the whole bowl. Then he unbuckled his sword and laid it on a stone slab at the centre of the floor and stepped back from it. It is a plain soldier's sword, and it has been working: the edge nicked along its whole length and honed back each time, the grip rewrapped more than once. Two years of the gate.

Then the senators came down. One by one they rose from their rows and walked down to the slab, took the sword up in both hands, spoke their oath to it, and laid it down again for the next. Some held it like soldiers and some like clerks, and one old woman held it a long while before she spoke, but every one of them came down. It took most of an hour, and the rows above did not empty.

The last to rise was the First Senator. She took up the sword, said nothing at all, and put it back into the dictator's hands.

Senator Contarin met me afterwards in a small room off the gallery. Before we spoke of a single breastplate, he asked whether I had seen it. I said I had. We agreed three hundred harnesses for the gate garrison before supper.
