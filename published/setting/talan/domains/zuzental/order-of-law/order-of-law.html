The Order of Law
Zuzental Sub-Region · Contemplative Order · The Court of One
You are the only court that can judge you.
✦

At a Glance

Etymology
Order of Law is plain Talanese from the hinge of the Golden and Dark Eras: the order of those who hold themselves to the law. The forest is Kyrrskog, far older than the Order; the monastery at its heart is the Ankerhold; the capital on the coast is Drukha.
Position
South-eastern Zuzental, the largest country in the domain. The Thousand Kingdom lies north, with Lograth on its mountain just across the line; the Legea Empire north-west, meeting it at the lake; the Namur Republic west, across a knot of mountains; the great river runs the whole southern border, with Crossroads on it at the tri-point and Harro Distiratsua beyond; the Hafra strait lies east, with the Emerald Isles' Middle and Southern Isles across it.
Terrain
Farm country and heath in the north, drying to sandy steppe in the south-west; river valleys and hill country between, with the mountains on the Namur line. In the east-centre stands Kyrrskog, a forest the size of a kingdom, with the Ankerhold at its heart. Drukha stands on the south-east coast at the river's mouth.

The Court of One

"I told them a week. I had a horse to sell in Drukha on the Tuesday. I came out on the forty-first day with two knots and the horse long sold by my brother, and I have never been so glad to have been wrong about how long anything takes."

Mathis Rolant Ferre, a horse-trader of the Thousand Kingdom, on the forest road

Anyone may walk into Kyrrskog and ask to sit. The road runs five days under the trees from the forest's edge to the Ankerhold, and the monks walk it with anyone who comes. At the Ankerhold the sitter is given a cell: stone, one door, one chair, a bare wall facing the chair. A monk passes food through a hatch twice a day and asks nothing. The sitter stays until they have weighed their own case, what they did, whom it cost, what is owed, and set a verdict on themselves. Most sit a week or two. Some sit a season.

The sitter is alone with the case and free to leave the cell. A bell by the door calls a listener, a monk who sits outside the hatch and asks questions and answers none. A sitter with a verdict takes it out to the courtyards; a sitter whose verdict breaks there goes back to the cell and sits again. Most walk between the cell and the courtyard two or three times before a verdict holds.

People come at the turns of a life. A widow after a death she thinks she caused. A captain before he takes a command. A man who drowned a boy on a dare forty years ago and was never caught. A queen's advocate who won a case she should have lost. They come from every domain, and more from abroad since the eastern line reached Crossroads and made the journey a matter of days.

The Contrary. A verdict set in a cell is taken out to the courtyards to be tested, because the Order holds only a verdict that is truly meant. There the sitter meets a Contrary, a monk trained to argue the other side. The Contrary stands, the sitter sits, and the Contrary claps each challenge down onto the flagstones: what did the boy's mother lose, and what did you give her? If the verdict is too soft, the Contrary argues for the wronged. If it is too hard, the Contrary argues for the sitter.

"The soft ones everyone expects. It's the hard ones that take the days. A man who wants forty years for a lie told to his father is not being honest, he's being proud, and I will clap at him until he sees it."

Pema Lowe, a Contrary of the Ankerhold

The courtyards are never quiet: the clapping carries under the trees at every hour, and pilgrims on the last mile of the road hear it before they see a wall. A verdict that survives its Contrary is knotted into a cord, and the sitter walks out with it.

Tsopa · the Courtyard Practice

The monks train on each other first. Every morning the chapter pairs off in the courtyards and argues its own cases, one standing and one seated, each taking the side against themselves; a novice is not made a Contrary until she can argue against her own verdict better than anyone else can.

✦

The Cord

The verdict is knotted into a sundu, a cord worn at the wrist: one knot for each year the sitter has set. The sentence is whatever the sitter set and the Contrary let stand, and it is served in the open. Years of labour on the Order's roads and dykes and on the harbour at Drukha. Restitution carried to the wronged's door, sometimes across three domains. A year of silence. A trade given up.

Each year the sentence is kept, a knot is cut at the Order's house in Drukha. When the last knot is cut the matter is closed, and in the Order's ground no one raises it again.

The Ever-Knot

A verdict larger than a life (a killing, a ruin that cannot be mended) cannot be paid in years. The sitter gives the life to the Order instead, and the cord carries a single knot that is never cut. The ever-knotted become monks of the Ankerhold. They stand beside the monks who took the vow with nothing owed, and the Order draws no line between the two.

What the cord is worth abroad. The Order holds a served cord as a closed matter. Other courts hold it as they please. A Thousand Kingdom judge may count the knots in mitigation; Lograth takes no notice of a cord at all, since a knot proves nothing a court can test; a Legean reader has no page for it. The Order hands no one over and shields no one: a court that wants a sitter may take him at the Order's border, and if he means his verdict he goes on serving it in their prison.

"You get two kinds on this line. The ones who hide the wrist, they're foreigners and it's their first year. The ones who don't, you can lend money to."

Dawa Ferris, a rail-conductor on the Crossroads line

What an Orderman Is Called

A child receives two names from a monk of the Order, drawn from the country's stock of a few hundred names. After sitting the Court of One, a person may set one of them down and take a name of their own choosing: anything at all except a name the monks give. It is the only choice about their name a person ever makes, and they make it about themselves. Many choose a word; some choose a foreign name; a few take the name of the person they wronged.

So a name tells you whether its bearer has sat. Sonam Rinchen has not. Sonam Weathers has, and chose Weathers the day he walked out. Marriage changes nothing, and the ever-knotted choose like everyone else.

The young. Village children who have not yet walked the forest road knot pear-cords for each other out of grass: three knots for a stolen pear, one for a lie about the dog. Parents pretend to be scandalised. The monks, who were children in the same villages, cut the grass knots with great ceremony when asked.

The Trimpon

The Order is ruled from the forest by the Trimpon, and the Trimpon is found.

When a Trimpon dies, the chapter sends monks out through the country's villages to look for a child of four or five who judges like the old: a child who hears two neighbours quarrel over a fence and says which of them is lying and why, and is right. The finders bring the candidates to the Ankerhold, and the chapter sets them the old test: a quarrel acted out in the courtyard by two monks, each in the right about half of it. The child who weighs both halves true is the Trimpon. The Order teaches that the forest recognises its own.

The found child is raised in the Ankerhold for fifteen years in judgment and in the country's business, while the Desi governs as regent. At twenty the Trimpon walks into a cell and sits the Court of One like anyone else, and comes out with a chosen name; it is the one day in a generation the whole country waits on a name. The present Trimpon was found in 2496 at the age of five as Dawa Lhamo, sat in 2511, and came out as Dawa Sorrel.

"We had the ferry flags up by noon. Nobody knew what she'd pick. My mother said a Trimpon always picks something you'd never guess and then it fits her like a glove for fifty years. Sorrel. We said it to each other all afternoon."

Choden Marrow, a ferry-hand of Drukha, on the day in 2511

The Trimpon owes nobody: no family, no house, no purse. The Trimpon seals the Order's treaties, names the Desi, and hears the cases the chapter cannot settle.

⚿   A Second Pass

The test finds a soul. An ever-knotted monk who dies with the verdict truly meant and still unpaid comes before Epairima in person at Dauria, because a soul that has judged itself that hard is a weighty soul, and sometimes the Judge of Souls issues her rare reversal verdict: the soul goes back to the Life Layer for a second pass, to pay the rest. The children who judge like the old are these souls, carrying the old verdict under the new life.

What the Order believes
The forest recognises its own
The chapter tests for a gift of judgment and finds it. The Order does not know what it finds.
What is true
Every Trimpon was an ever-knot
Each Trimpon is a returned ever-knot still paying. The Trimpon feels it and cannot name it: the weight of a debt with no remembered cause.
The chosen name at twenty is the one trace. Trimpons tend to choose, without knowing why, the name they chose in the life before. The present Trimpon's Sorrel was also the chosen name of an ever-knotted monk of the Ankerhold who died in 2490, the year before her birth, and the chapter's own register holds both names.

Drukha and the Desi

"The ferry wasn't until the second tide, so I went to see what everyone here goes to see. The Order's house on the quay opens at first light and the line is already down to the water: a fishwife, two dockers, a gentleman in a better coat than mine, every one of them with a hand held out. A monk takes the wrist, finds the knot, and cuts it with a little pair of brass shears, and the knot drops into a bowl. That is all of it. Nobody says a word. But I watched the gentleman come out onto the quay after, and he stood there rubbing his wrist like a man who had set down a trunk, and then he bought the whole fish-stall breakfast."

Gilles Aubert Marchal, a cloth-factor of the Thousand Kingdom, in a letter to his wife, waiting on the Middle Isle ferry at Drukha

Drukha stands at the mouth of the great river on the south-east coast, a port of about ninety thousand where everything that crosses the Order's country arrives or leaves. The eastern line from Merkavar crosses into Zuzental at Crossroads and runs north through the Order's country toward Lograth, and a branch follows the river down to the Drukha yards; the ferry for Middle Isle and Oathmoore leaves from the harbour wall every tide; the river-barges come down from the west. The Order's house in Drukha stands on the quay, and the cutting of knots happens in its hall, in public, every morning of the year.

The country is governed from here. The Desi is the first lay officer, named by the Trimpon, and regent while the Trimpon is a child. Under the Desi the Seneschals keep the districts: the roads, the dykes, the granaries, the courts of ordinary law, the works. A foreign envoy is received by the Desi in Drukha; a treaty is sealed by the Trimpon in Kyrrskog; a new bridge is planned by the Seneschals and built by the sentenced.

No one judges others who has not first judged themselves. Any lay person may be a Seneschal who has sat the Court of One, whatever the verdict; a clean sitting counts as fully as five knots. A candidate's sitting is argued harder in the courtyards than anyone else's, because a verdict of nothing owed from someone who plainly owes is exactly what a Contrary is there to break.

"They clapped at me for eleven days about my uncle's mill. I'd have told you I owed nothing. I owed him an apology and a roof, and I've paid both, and I'm a better Seneschal for it."

Norbu Keelan, Seneschal of the river district

The works are the country's great enterprise. The Order's roads are the best kept in Zuzental, its river dykes hold, and the Drukha harbour wall has been rebuilt three times by people paying off their own verdicts. Materials come from the Order's tithe, and the labour from the cords of some forty thousand sitters serving at any one time.

Kyrrskog and the Ankerhold

Kyrrskog is quiet the way deep water is quiet. The trees are old and tall and close, the ground is soft, and sound dies a few yards off the road.

The Ankerhold at its heart began as one cell. A hermit walked into the forest about 1330 MR, as the Golden Empire fell, and sat in a stone cell he built himself; he called himself Anker and never said what he owed. His cord hangs in the Ankerhold with one knot uncut, the first ever-knot. Others came to sit, and some stayed. Through the Dark Era the self-sentenced kept the roads open and the dykes up when no crown in the south could, and the country gathered around the only institution still working; by the end of the dark the Order was the state.

Today the Ankerhold is a town of stone under the trees: some six thousand monks, the rows of cells, the courtyards, the chapter-house, and the Trimpon's hall at the centre.

Everyone in the country has a theory. He was an Imperial general who lost the south. He was the Emperor's own physician. He was the man who lit the first lamp in the mines in 1321. Children say he is still sitting in his first cell, and that the knot on his cord is warm.

✦

Peoples

Elves, Humans, and Hobgoblins live throughout the country, and each sits the Court in its own way. Elves sit longest, and the Contraries say they argue with the dead. Humans sit fast and come back, twice or three times in a life. Hobgoblins set the hardest verdicts, and their Contraries spend more days arguing a Hobgoblin down than anyone else; a Hobgoblin with his last knot due will sometimes ask for it not to be cut.

✦

Crossroads

Crossroads stands inside the Order's ground, on the southern river, and the Order has never ruled it. The Seneschal of the south district keeps a house in the town and collects no toll. The town's three bridges are kept by the sentenced, and travellers crossing to the Spider's Silk Inn walk past men and women repointing the stonework with their knots at their wrists.

Live Tension · Lograth Wants to Hear the Contraries

Every sitter speaks their whole case aloud to a Contrary, and some of those cases are crimes a Lograth court is still trying to prove. Lograth's advocates have petitioned the Concord of Courts to compel a Contrary to testify. The Order holds that what is said in the courtyards belongs to the sitter; Lograth holds that a truth kept from a court is a seal, and every seal can be petitioned. The Concord has not ruled, and three Contraries have been named in the petition.
